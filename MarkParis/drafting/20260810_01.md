Mark walked out of the coffee shop into the main spine of the space station.  

Elsinore was originally designed as a large spinning ring station, about a kilometer from center to rim, spinning once every minute to provide a little less than one Earth gravity.  The station was only partially built, and new construction was constantly ongoing.  To keep the whole structure balanced, it was being built in two sectors, one on each limb of the ring.  The rest of the ring was little more than scaffolding.

The coffee shop, apparently called "Bard's" by the sign over the door, was located near an end of one of the sectors.  When Mark looked right, the walkway terminated at a door with a guy sitting on a box nearby.  Probably guarding against curious kids.  Towards the left lay the rest of what served for civilization two and a half light hours away from the sun.

People were milling about, going about their business, dodging numerous animal-like drones doing whatever they do.  Two guys dressed in thick beige overalls covered with pockets wheeled some large rectangular thing past Mark on the way to the construction zone, probably a section of inner wall wired up for some function.  As they passed by, one of the guys looked up at Mark with what looked like an expression of suspicion or disdain.

There were maybe 250 people on the station at any one time, electricians, plumbers, various other engineers who maintained and expanded the structure; miners who either worked in the Uranus system or made jaunts out to the trans-Neptune system; scientists who considered themselves lucky to be stationed at such a special place; the odd bureaucrat who considered themselves supremely unlucky to have deserved such a promotion.  And the families some of them brought along for the assignment.

Word travels fast in a community like this.

Marked headed away from construction and towards the lift that would bring him to his quarters, about mid-way between rim and hub.  As he walked, people looked up at him now and then.  He passed by a group of ice miners, wrapped up in their fleeces and watching something on a screen propped up on a random storage box.  They stopped their low chattering and glared at him with what Mark could hardly mischaracterize as disdain.

What's with these assholes, thought Mark.

When he finally got back to the tube transport, finding it hard to shrug off the weight of all the uncomfortable stares, he encountered the egress operator.

"Hi, Mark Paris, headed up to my quarters on 5."

"So, you're Paris," the operator said, finding his information in a touch screen device.  "Looks like you're stuck here with us for a while, huh?"

"Yep, looks like it," said Mark.

"Let me give you some advice."  Mark just wanted to head up to his bunk to think.  "Don't try any dumb shit that could compromise this vessel.  There are 263 people here who depend on the boring, redundant safety habits of everyone else.  We don't mess around with protocols here for some personal curiosity."

The operator then smacked the passage card on Mark's chest, which then fell into Mark's hand.  "Oh, ok, thanks," was all he could think of to say.  With that, the door opened, and he slunk in, hopeful for a few moments of privacy.
